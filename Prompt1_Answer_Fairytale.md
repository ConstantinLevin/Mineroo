Should the Local GPU-OCR Orchestrator Exist? A Documentation-Precision Verdict

TL;DR


Verdict: The orchestrator should NOT be built as designed. RunPod's own queue-based serverless endpoint absorbs almost all of the pain — its built-in queue holds jobs through arbitrarily long (8-min+) cold starts (bounded only by a 24-hour default job TTL, not the 2-minute "timed out waiting for worker" drop that burned you on the load-balancing endpoint), scales flex workers from zero, retries, and supports EU-only datacenters. The local side collapses to submit → poll/webhook → collect, plus a cost/budget guard.
Queue-based is the correct RunPod mode you never properly evaluated: it needs a handler.py, one job = one document, S3-compatible storage for the large PDFs/results, host-level model caching so your image stays small, and flex-only workers (min=0) so there is genuinely zero idle cost — the standby/warm-pool trap on the LB endpoint does not apply to flex workers.
If you want to leave RunPod for a stronger EU-sovereignty story or better ergonomics, the credible 2026 alternatives are Baseten (best-in-class async/queue inference API with webhooks, but weak on hard EU residency), Beam (native task_queue with retries/callbacks, scale-to-zero), Cerebrium (Dockerfile + region pinning), and for genuine EU legal sovereignty Scaleway/OVH/Northflank-in-EU — but none is a clean drop-in that beats queue-based RunPod on effort, and pure-EU-sovereign providers lack a mature scale-to-zero job-queue primitive.


Key Findings

(A) RunPod queue-based serverless — verified at documentation precision

The mode you rejected early (because your image was handler-less) is the one that solves your problem. Every load-bearing claim below is from RunPod's official docs.


Two endpoint types, explicitly contrasted. RunPod docs: "Queue-based endpoints use a built-in queueing system with guaranteed execution and automatic retries. Ideal for async tasks, batch processing, and long-running jobs. Implemented using handler functions. Load balancing endpoints route traffic directly to workers, bypassing the queue." You are currently on the load-balancing type — the one that drops requests waiting >2 min for a worker. The 2-minute drop is a property of load-balancing endpoints, not queue-based ones. RunpodRunpod
Queued jobs survive arbitrary cold starts. On a queue-based endpoint, a submitted job enters IN_QUEUE and waits for a worker; there is no "timed out waiting for worker" short-drop. The only hard limit is the job TTL: "Total lifespan of the job. Timer starts when submitted and covers queue time, execution time, and everything in between." Default TTL is 86,400,000 ms (24 hours), min 10 s, max 7 days. So a burst of 15×200-page documents fired at a cold, scaled-to-zero endpoint will queue and drain as workers warm — the exact failure you hit on the LB endpoint does not recur. Caveat: "TTL is a hard limit. If TTL expires while a job is running, the job is immediately removed and status checks return 404." runpodrunpod
Endpoints and I/O. /run (async, non-blocking; results retained 30 min), /runsync (sync; results retained 1 min, 5 min max), /status (poll), /stream, /cancel, /retry, /purge-queue, /health. Webhooks are first-class: add a webhook URL to the payload and RunPod POSTs the result JSON on completion; "If the call fails, Runpod retries up to 2 more times with a 10-second delay." runpod
Payload/result size limits and the sanctioned large-I/O pattern. Per RunPod's handler documentation, "Only /run is limited to 10MB, /runsync is limited to 20MB" (docs.runpod.io/serverless/workers/handlers/overview). RunPod support is unambiguous on the workaround: "Upload your photos to cloud storage and your serverless workers can download from a link. The limits are fixed and there is no way around them." The s3Config block is the sanctioned per-request mechanism ("Works with any S3-compatible provider including MinIO, Backblaze B2, and DigitalOcean Spaces"), and network volumes mount at /runpod-volume. For 30k scanned PDFs this means: PDFs live in an EU S3-compatible bucket; each job payload carries only a key; the handler downloads the PDF, runs MinerU, and writes results back to the bucket. runpodRunpod
Retry when a worker dies mid-job. Queue-based endpoints are documented as providing "guaranteed execution and automatic retries." A TIMED_OUT state occurs "if the worker fails to report back a result for the job before it reaches its timeout threshold." A manual POST /retry/{job_id} requeues a FAILED or TIMED_OUT job with the same job ID and original input. The docs do not spell out the automatic-requeue count/algorithm for a mid-job worker death, so treat automatic retry as advertised-but-underspecified and plan to call /retry explicitly for terminal states. Runpod + 2
Billing — what is and isn't charged. "You're billed from when a worker starts until it fully stops, rounded up to the nearest second." Three billed phases: (1) Start time (container init + model load — cold start IS billed), (2) Execution time, (3) Idle-timeout duration (default 5 s a worker stays warm after a job — this IS billed). Crucially, time a job merely sits IN_QUEUE with no worker running is not billed. runpod + 4
No standby trap on flex workers. The ~$12/11-worker-hours idle bill you hit came from active/always-on workers. Flex workers "Scale to zero when idle" at "Standard per-second rate"; active workers are "Always running (24/7)" and "incur charges continuously, including when idle." The REST field workersMin (active count) defaults to 0 and "will always stay running… and will be charged even if no requests are being processed." Set active/min workers = 0, use flex only → genuinely zero idle cost. This is the single configuration change that neutralizes the cost trap. runpod + 3
FlashBoot. Enabled by default on new GPU and CPU endpoints, retains worker state after spin-down for faster revival, and is not listed as a separate billed line item (no surcharge documented). It reduces — but does not guarantee elimination of — the billable Start-time phase. runpod
Host-level model caching means you stop baking the model into the 20 GB image. RunPod's cached-models feature pre-loads Hugging Face model files onto the host: "When you select a cached model for your endpoint, Runpod automatically tries to start your workers on hosts that already contain the selected model. If no cached host are available, the system delays starting your workers until the model is downloaded… ensuring you still won't be charged for the download time." Files land at /runpod-volume/huggingface-cache/hub/… and load "significantly faster than the same model loaded from a network volume." This directly attacks your ~8-min cold start (20 GB pull + model load). Limitation: cached models must be Hugging Face-hosted; a private non-HF model must still be baked in. Runpod
EU-only datacenters are supported on queue-based endpoints. The dataCenterIds filter accepts EU IDs; RunPod's EU serverless regions include EU-RO-1, EU-CZ-1, EU-FR-1, EU-NL-1, EU-SE-1, EUR-IS-1/2/3, EUR-NO-1. A documented example endpoint pins "dataCenterIds": "EU-NL-1,EU-RO-1,EU-SE-1" with "scalerType": "QUEUE_DELAY" and "workersMin": 0 — i.e., exactly an EU-only, scale-to-zero, queue-based endpoint. Data-residency caveat below. RunpodRunpod
2025–2026 changes that matter. RunPod shipped (i) run-batch — a batch-inference submission mode with an hours-scale SLA where "Runpod schedules the work as capacity frees up. You don't manage workers at all" — purpose-built for throughput jobs like yours; (ii) Flash (GA April 30, 2026), an open-source Python SDK that deploys a decorated function to serverless with "no Dockerfile, no container registry," supporting both "Queue-based processing [for] batch and async workloads" and load-balanced endpoints; and (iii) host-level model caching. Per RunPod's January 20, 2026 press release, the company had "surpassed $120 million in annual recurring revenue," served "more than 500,000 developers" "across 31 global regions," and describes "sub-500 millisecond cold start times" (a warm/FlashBoot best case, not a cold 20 GB pull). By its June 24, 2026 Summit Partners round ($100M at a ~$1.0B valuation), RunPod reported more than one million developers and roughly $240M annualized revenue. RunPod reports SOC 2 Type II. Runpod


Net for (A): every measured pain point is addressed by queue-based + flex-only + S3 I/O + model caching. Queue-until-warm, readiness gating, and park-when-idle are handled by the platform. The only local logic that survives is submit/track/collect and a budget guard.

Details

Why the local orchestrator's raison d'être evaporates on queue-based RunPod

Your planned components map one-to-one onto platform features:


Durable job queue → RunPod's built-in queue (IN_QUEUE, 24 h TTL).
Queue-until-warm → inherent: jobs wait in queue through cold start; no 2-min drop.
Readiness gating (GET /v1/models == 200) → unnecessary: you submit jobs, not raw HTTP to vLLM; the queue schedules onto workers when ready.
Park-when-idle → flex workers scale to zero automatically.
Hard cost budget → still yours to enforce (see Recommendations), plus maxWorkers as a concurrency/cost cap and executionTimeout to kill runaways.
Crash recovery → /status + /retry for terminal states; webhooks remove most polling.


What remains local is a thin driver: enumerate the 30k PDFs, upload to an EU bucket, submit one job per document, record job IDs, receive webhooks (or poll /status), collect results, and stop submitting when a spend threshold is hit.

The MinerU execution-mode implication

Queue-based requires a handler in the image. Your current vlm-http-client mode fires OpenAI-compatible requests at a remote vLLM server; that architecture is what pushed you to the load-balancing endpoint. For queue-based, wrap MinerU's in-process/engine mode (pure-VLM backend) inside handler.py: one job = one document, handler renders pages, runs the VLM, writes output to S3. vLLM still runs inside the worker; you simply stop exposing it as an HTTP surface and let RunPod's queue be the front door. This is a real but contained code change — smaller than building the whole orchestrator.

EU data-residency: the precision the task demands

There is a hard distinction between data residency (where servers physically are) and data sovereignty (whose law governs the operator). GDPR does not mandate localization, but the cleanest compliance path for tax PII is EU processing. RunPod, Modal, and Baseten are US-incorporated: pinning EU datacenters satisfies residency but leaves theoretical US CLOUD Act exposure — potentially acceptable for a family office with a signed DPA and SCCs, but not "sovereign." Scaleway and OVH (French) and Northflank-into-EU-VPC give both residency and sovereignty. If your tax/family-office client's risk posture treats any US-operator exposure as a dealbreaker, that pushes you off RunPod entirely and toward Scaleway/OVH/Northflank — at the cost of rebuilding orchestration. If a DPA + EU-region pin + SCCs suffice (the common enterprise posture), queue-based RunPod on EU-only datacenters is defensible. Verify RunPod offers a signed DPA before committing production PII.

Recommendations

Stage 1 — Cheap decisive experiment (½–1 day, <$20). Before writing any orchestrator, build a minimal handler.py (MinerU pure-VLM in-process), bake nothing but code, attach the model via host-level caching (or a network volume if the model isn't HF-hosted), deploy a queue-based endpoint pinned to EU datacenters, flex workers only (workersMin=0), maxWorkers=10, S3 config pointing at an EU bucket. Then fire the exact burst that broke you before: 15 documents × ~200 pages at a fully cold, scaled-to-zero endpoint. Measure: (a) do all 15 jobs survive the ~8-min cold start in IN_QUEUE? (b) end-to-end wall clock; (c) itemized bill, confirming no idle charge after idleTimeout. This single test settles the go/no-go.

Stage 2 — If Stage 1 passes (expected). Do NOT build the durable-queue/readiness-gating/park-when-idle orchestrator. Build instead a ~200-line async client: upload → submit (/run) → webhook receiver (or /status poll) → collect → /retry on FAILED/TIMED_OUT. Keep only the budget guard as bespoke logic: track cumulative billed seconds via /health + your own accounting, and stop submitting new jobs past a hard EUR ceiling. Evaluate run-batch for the bulk 30k pass since it explicitly trades latency for hands-off throughput scheduling.

Stage 3 — Sovereignty escalation path. If legal review rejects US-operator exposure, port the same handler.py/container to Northflank BYOC into an EU VPC (keeps orchestration managed while data stays in your cloud) or Scaleway GPU + a small self-managed queue. Budget for reintroducing the orchestration you avoided on RunPod. If you want maximum EU-native serverless ergonomics, trial Koyeb (Paris/Frankfurt, scale-to-zero) but confirm its GPU cold-start behavior and post-Mistral roadmap first.

Benchmarks that change the recommendation:


If Stage-1 shows jobs are dropped before workers warm (contradicting the docs), or TTL/execution-timeout interacts badly with 200-page documents → keep a thin local retry/queue shim, but still not the full orchestrator.
If per-document cold-start billing dominates cost at 10 workers → add 1–2 active workers during the bulk run (accept some idle cost) or use run-batch.
If legal rejects DPA+SCC+EU-pin → jump to Stage 3.


Caveats


Automatic-retry algorithm is underspecified. RunPod advertises "guaranteed execution and automatic retries" for queue-based endpoints but does not document the retry count/behavior on mid-job worker death; the only precisely documented mechanism is manual /retry for FAILED/TIMED_OUT jobs. Plan to call it explicitly. Runpod
TTL is a hard delete. A 24-h default TTL covers queue + execution; a 200-page document that queues long behind a burst and runs slowly could in principle approach limits — set ttl and executionTimeout generously (both max 7 days) and confirm in Stage 1.
Model caching requires HF-hosted models. If your MinerU 2.5 VLM weights are not on Hugging Face, host-level caching doesn't apply and you must bake the model in (keeping the cold-start problem) or use a network volume (slower than cache). Confirm the model's hosting before assuming small images.
Residency ≠ sovereignty. EU datacenter pinning on RunPod/Modal/Baseten does not remove US CLOUD Act theoretical exposure. This is a legal judgment call for tax PII; get the DPA and a sign-off.
Some marketing numbers are aspirational. "Sub-500 ms cold starts" (RunPod's Jan 20, 2026 press release) and "48% under 200ms" describe warm/FlashBoot best cases on popular images, not a cold 20 GB pull + 1.2B-param VLM load; your realistic cold start remains minutes, which is precisely why queue-survival (not cold-start elimination) is the property that matters.
Competitor cold-start and pricing figures are drawn from vendor and third-party pages dated 2025–2026 and fluctuate; verify current GPU rates and EU region availability in each console before committing.
Vast.ai and Salad were excluded for GDPR tax-PII processing because they run on distributed third-party/community hosts with weak residency guarantees, not because of price or capability.
